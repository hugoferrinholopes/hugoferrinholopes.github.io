About

Bio

I am the FLAD Visiting Professor at Georgetown University. In January 2027, I will join the School of Economics, Management and Political Science at the University of Minho as an assistant professor of political science.

I am also an associate researcher at the Institute of Social Sciences, University of Lisbon, where I earned my PhD in September 2025. Previously, I enjoyed research stays at the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the European University Institute and at the Institute of Public Goods and Policies at the Spanish National Research Council (IPP-CSIC).

I study comparative political behavior and institutions, including parties, representation, elections, and youth politics. I am interested in how people, and young people in particular, act within democratic institutions and how their actions change those institutions in return. A key question running through my work is how far democratic institutions can stretch before even their most loyal supporters begin to break away. I address it across three strands.

First, I study divisions inside parties and youth wings. Conventional theories hold that divided parties lose votes and that transferring power to members and youth wings could push mainstream parties away from the center. I argue that this view often overlooks how the rank and file actually decide. They do not simply stick to their policy goals. Instead, they rationalize choices that bring their party closer to power, adjusting their reading of the party brand to fit those choices. If that is the case, the question is not whether a party is divided, but whether shared rules give divisions a way to be expressed without breaking the party apart. Where they do, internal debate can make a party stronger rather than weaker.

Second, I examine the limits that members and voters set from below, both inside parties and at the ballot box. Scholars disagree on whether parties, and the party systems they form, are led from the top or constrained from below. I argue that, even in leader-centered parties, the grassroots keep an advantage when it comes to accountability. Leaders depend on broad support, while any single member or voter can withdraw theirs at little cost. That imbalance gives the grassroots leverage and draws boundaries that leaders must respect. Accountability, in this sense, works not only through the support people give but also through the support they can take away.

Third, I look at how young people gain access to political institutions, what they learn once inside, and how institutions open or close the way for them. I pay particular attention to party youth wings, which often serve as the first stage of a political career. While much of the debate focuses on formal rules, I argue that opening institutions to a new generation depends less on what the rules allow than on how institutions work in practice. Access also shapes what newcomers learn once inside, much as early voting experiences shape lifelong habits, and it may draw young political elites away from the generation they are meant to represent.

Research agenda

  • Political behavior

    • Why do voters and party members support a party, and what leads them to change their minds?
    • How do people react to party policy shifts?

    All publications on political behavior

  • Parties and party systems

    • How do parties organize themselves, and who holds power inside them?
    • How do party systems change when new parties emerge and established ones decline?

    All publications on parties and party systems

  • Political representation

    • Who reaches political office, and who is left out?
    • Do representatives act on the commitments they made to the people who elected them?

    All publications on political representation

  • Youth in politics

    • How do young people take part in politics, inside and outside institutions?
    • How do youth wings respond when their party compromises on policy?
